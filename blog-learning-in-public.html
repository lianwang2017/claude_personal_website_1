Learning in Public

Jan 5, 2026 · 6 min read · by Jane Wang
📖

There's something terrifying about pressing "publish" on something you've just learned. What if you're wrong? What if someone more experienced calls you out? What if it's obvious to everyone else?

Despite these fears, I've decided to embrace learning in public—sharing my journey, my mistakes, and my discoveries as they happen. Here's why.

The Traditional Learning Model is Broken

We're taught to learn in private, master a skill, and only then share our expertise. This model made sense in a slower world, but it doesn't reflect how learning actually works in 2026.

Learning isn't linear. It's messy, recursive, full of dead ends and sudden breakthroughs. By waiting until we're "experts" to share, we miss out on the most valuable part of the journey—the struggle, the questions, the incremental progress.

Why Learn in Public?

1. It Accelerates Your Learning

When you know you're going to explain something publicly, you learn it more deeply. You can't gloss over the details or fool yourself into thinking you understand something you don't. Teaching forces clarity.

Plus, sharing your work invites feedback. Someone might point out a better approach, a common pitfall, or a resource you didn't know existed. This feedback loop is invaluable.

2. It Builds Your Network

Learning in public attracts like-minded people. When you share your journey, you find others on similar paths. Some are ahead of you and can offer guidance. Others are behind and benefit from your insights. Both connections are valuable.

I've connected with more interesting people through sharing my learning than through any other activity. These aren't just professional contacts—they're genuine relationships built on mutual growth.

3. It Creates a Personal Knowledge Base

Your public learning becomes a resource for your future self. How many times have you solved a problem, only to encounter it again months later and not remember the solution? When you document your learning publicly, you're creating a searchable archive of your growth.

4. It Helps Others

This might sound presumptuous, but hear me out: your beginner's perspective is valuable. When you're learning something new, you remember what confused you, what clicked, what resources helped. Experts often forget what it's like to be a beginner.

Your struggle might be exactly what someone else needs to see to keep going.

Overcoming the Fear

Let's address the elephant in the room: imposter syndrome. The fear that you're not qualified, that you'll be exposed as a fraud, that you have nothing valuable to contribute.

Here's what I've learned: everyone feels this way. Even experts. The difference is that people who learn in public have learned to share despite the fear, not in the absence of it.

A few mindset shifts that helped me:

  • You don't need to be an expert to share: You just need to be one step ahead of someone else.
  • Being wrong is part of the process: If someone corrects you, that's a gift, not an attack.
  • Progress over perfection: A messy post that helps one person is better than a perfect post that never gets published.
  • Your perspective is unique: Even if the topic has been covered before, it hasn't been covered by you, with your experiences and insights.

How to Start

You don't need a blog, a YouTube channel, or a big following to learn in public. Start small:

  • Tweet about what you learned today
  • Write a README for a project explaining your approach
  • Answer questions on forums or Stack Overflow
  • Share a "TIL" (Today I Learned) on social media
  • Keep a public learning log

The medium doesn't matter. What matters is the act of sharing, of being vulnerable, of contributing to the collective knowledge.

What I've Learned So Far

I'm still early in my learning-in-public journey, but already I've noticed:

People are kinder than you expect. The internet has a reputation for harshness, but in my experience, most people are supportive, especially when you're clearly trying to learn and grow.

You learn more than you think. It's easy to feel like you're not making progress, but when you document your journey, you can look back and see how far you've come.

The process matters more than the product. The act of sharing, reflecting, and engaging is where the real value lies, not in the number of likes or views you get.

An Invitation

If you've been hesitating to share your learning journey, consider this your sign to start. Pick one thing you learned this week and share it somewhere—anywhere. See how it feels.

You might be surprised by what happens.
